Rob Gronkowski is the greatest tight end of all time, and it’s not close enough to justify the hours people spend pretending otherwise. If I need one tight end to win a football game, I’m taking Gronk before you finish explaining your spreadsheet.

Travis Kelce and Tony Gonzalez belong in the conversation. They just don’t change my answer, because nobody combined receiving dominance, destructive blocking and championship moments like Gronk.

\## Gronk played the entire position

The best tight end of all time should probably be excellent at everything a tight end does. Apparently, that becomes a controversial requirement whenever somebody brings up blocking.

Gronk could split out wide, beat a defensive back and catch a touchdown through contact. On the next possession, he could line up beside the tackle and help move a grown man somewhere that grown man did not want to go.

That mattered to the New England Patriots offense. Keeping Gronk on the field meant they could run or throw without announcing their intentions through a substitution.

And the receiving numbers weren’t exactly modest. In 2011, he caught 90 passes for 1,327 yards and 17 touchdowns, setting the NFL single-season record for touchdown receptions by a tight end.

He added a rushing touchdown, too, bringing his total to 18. Apparently, humiliating defenses through the air wasn’t keeping him busy enough.

\*\*That is the standard:\*\* elite receiving production while doing the physical work the position demands. Blocking deserves more consideration than a polite mention before everyone returns to counting catches.

\## Kansas City already watched the answer

Go back to the AFC Championship Game at Arrowhead in January 2019. Gronk was near the end of his Patriots career, and nobody watching him move was confusing him with the fresh-legged 2011 version.

He still delivered six catches for 79 yards in New England’s 37–31 overtime win. More importantly, he delivered when a failed possession could have ended the season.

His 25-yard catch over Eric Berry on third-and-five helped set up the Patriots’ late fourth-quarter touchdown. In overtime, he converted another third down with a 15-yard reception on the winning drive.

Kansas City knew who he was, knew what was coming and still couldn’t get him off the field. That’s a fairly convincing scouting report.

\> When the season was on the line, Gronk was still the matchup New England trusted to win.

And this wasn’t some isolated evening people drag out because the highlights look nice. His Patriots career was packed with games where coverage looked reasonable until the ball arrived and everyone remembered how large he was.

He caught nine passes for 116 yards and two touchdowns against Philadelphia in Super Bowl LII, even though New England lost. Against the Rams in Super Bowl LIII, his 29-yard catch put the Patriots at the two-yard line before the game’s only touchdown.

Different defenses, different circumstances, same miserable assignment. Somebody had to cover No. 87, and somebody generally needed help.

\## Tampa Bay got more than a retirement tour

Then Gronk retired, came back and helped the Tampa Bay Buccaneers win a championship. A perfectly normal thing to do after your body has already spent years collecting NFL collision damage.

Against Kansas City in Super Bowl LV, he caught six passes for 67 yards and scored Tampa Bay’s first two touchdowns in a 31–9 demolition. The Chiefs probably would have preferred he stayed retired.

Across two regular seasons with the Buccaneers, he produced 100 catches, 1,425 yards and 13 touchdowns. Tampa got a productive tight end who could still punish a defense on the biggest stage.

Yes, Tom Brady helped. Having the greatest quarterback ever is a lovely workplace benefit, but Brady wasn’t catching those contested passes or handling Gronk’s blocking assignments.

You can prefer another tight end’s longevity or career totals. I watched Gronk give an offense more ways to beat people, then keep delivering the plays that decided championships.

Give me No. 87, and let the other sideline figure out who’s volunteering.
